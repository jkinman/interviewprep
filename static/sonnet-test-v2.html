01

Recruiter Screen

20–30 min
Pass filters: eligible, motivated, comp-aligned, can communicate.
  • Open each answer with a one-line headline
  • Keep stories under 90 seconds
  • Name numbers: years, users, tech stack
  • End on "why this company" with real research
✗ DON'T ramble or bad-mouth your last employer.
02

Behavioral Loop

45–60 min
Evidence on rubric: ownership, collaboration, judgment, learning.
  • Use STAR for every story
  • Say "I" not "we" in Action
  • Name the result with a metric or outcome
  • Show the lesson or behavior change
✗ DON'T invent heroics, blame others, or skip the result.
BANK

Story Bank

3 stories
Reuse your best stories across multiple questions.
  • Lead with your LLM gateway story
  • Keep confirmed facts ready
  • Fill ADD DETAIL gaps before the interview
  • Practice aloud to stay under 2 minutes
✗ DON'T invent metrics or team sizes on the fly.
03

Your Questions

5–10 min
Show you've researched; ask what you really need to know.
  • Ask about the team's AI roadmap
  • Probe success metrics for the role
  • Ask how they balance speed and quality
  • End with timeline or next steps
✗ DON'T ask questions Google can answer.

RECRUITER SCREEN

A1

TELL ME ABOUT YOURSELF

Walk me through your background

60–90s
Really asking: Can you communicate clearly? Are you at the right level? Does your experience match the role?
COMMUNICATION LEVEL CHECK

What they are really screening for:

Eligibility and level match. They want to confirm you can do the job (8 years, senior IC), that you can explain technical work to a non-technical listener, and that you are not over- or under-qualified.

I'm a senior full-stack engineer with 8 years building production systems in React, TypeScript, and Node. Most recently I designed and shipped an internal LLM gateway and AI features at Highspot.
SPOKEN DRAFT (click to expand)

I'm a senior full-stack engineer with 8 years building production systems in React, TypeScript, and Node.

I joined Highspot in 2023 and spent the last three years working on AI infrastructure and features. I designed and built an internal LLM gateway—so the architecture, tech choices, and rollout approach—and then led the front-end work for AI-powered features that went into the product.

Before that, I earlier experience: companies, roles, and key outcomes. I've always been drawn to the intersection of infrastructure and user-facing features—building systems that are robust under the hood but feel seamless to users.

I'm looking now because I want to work on AI products at scale, and Acme's focus on specific Acme product or mission from research is exactly the kind of problem I want to dig into next.

Pitfalls:

  • Going chronologically from college—start with your current role
  • Listing every technology you've ever touched
  • Rambling past 90 seconds
  • Ending without connecting to "why this company"
✗ DON'T recite your resume line-by-line or talk for three minutes.
A2

WHY ARE YOU LOOKING?

Why are you leaving, or why now?

30–60s
Really asking: Are you a flight risk? Were you fired? Are you leaving for the right reasons?
MOTIVATION FLIGHT RISK

What they are really screening for:

Positive motivation and low risk. They want to hear you are leaving toward something (growth, new challenges, mission alignment), not running away from conflict or failure. If you were laid off or let go, they want to hear you own it calmly.

I'm looking because I've built the foundation for AI features at Highspot, and I'm ready for the next chapter—shipping AI products at scale with a team that's fully committed to that mission.
SPOKEN DRAFT (click to expand)

I'm looking because I've built the foundation for AI features at Highspot, and I'm ready for the next chapter—shipping AI products at scale with a team that's fully committed to that mission.

Over the last three years I designed and launched our LLM gateway and contributed to the AI features that are now in production. It's been a great run—a specific win or learning—but Highspot is company direction, pivot, or scope change that makes this a natural exit.

I want to join a team where AI is the core product, not a feature. Acme's work on specific Acme product or technical challenge is exactly the kind of problem I want to own end-to-end.

Pitfalls:

  • Bad-mouthing your manager or company
  • Saying "I'm bored" without naming what you want next
  • Blaming others for why you are leaving
  • Being vague—"looking for growth" with no specifics
✗ DON'T trash your employer or sound like you're running away from something.
A3

WHY ACME?

Why this company / why this role?

45–60s
Really asking: Did you do any research? Are you genuinely interested, or is this just another application?
MOTIVATION RESEARCH

What they are really screening for:

Genuine interest and preparation. They want to hear you've researched the company, understand the product or mission, and can connect your experience to their needs. Generic answers ("I've heard great things") are a red flag.

I'm excited about Acme because you're building AI-first products, not bolting AI onto an existing platform—and that aligns perfectly with the work I want to do next.
SPOKEN DRAFT (click to expand)

I'm excited about Acme because you're building AI-first products, not bolting AI onto an existing platform—and that aligns perfectly with the work I want to do next.

I've spent the last three years at Highspot building the infrastructure for AI features—designing the LLM gateway, integrating models, and shipping front-end experiences. That taught me a specific lesson or technical insight, and I want to apply that in an environment where AI is the core mission.

What stood out to me about Acme is specific product, technical challenge, team structure, or company value from research—e.g., "your approach to real-time LLM orchestration," "the blog post on prompt caching," "the team's work on latency optimization". That's the kind of problem I geek out on, and I'd love to contribute at this stage of growth.

Plus, the senior IC role here feels like the right fit—something from the job description that matches your experience, e.g., "owning features end-to-end," "working across the stack," "collaborating with PM and design".

Pitfalls:

  • Saying "I've heard great things" with no specifics
  • Focusing only on what you want ("I want to learn") instead of what you bring
  • Naming competitors or other companies you're interviewing with
  • Faking enthusiasm for a mission you don't understand
✗ DON'T give a generic answer that could apply to any company in the space.
A4

SALARY EXPECTATIONS

What are your salary expectations?

20–40s
Really asking: Are you in our budget? Will we waste time if we move you forward?
COMP FIT BUDGET

What they are really screening for:

Budget alignment. They want a number or range so they can decide whether to continue. Saying "I'm flexible" or "What's the range?" without giving your anchor first often results in a lowball or disqualification.

Based on my 8 years of experience and the senior IC scope, I'm targeting your number or range, and currency—e.g., "$160k–$180k USD base," or "around $170k base". I'm open to discussing total comp once I understand the equity and benefits structure.
SPOKEN DRAFT (click to expand)

Based on my 8 years of experience and the senior IC scope, I'm targeting your number or range, and currency.

That's anchored on a brief rationale—e.g., "market data for senior full-stack engineers in Vancouver," "my current comp plus a step up for the new scope," "conversations with other companies at this stage".

I'm open to discussing total comp once I understand the equity and benefits structure. If Acme's range is different, I'd love to hear it—I'm more interested in the right role and team than hitting a specific number.

Pitfalls:

  • Saying "I'm flexible" or "Whatever you think is fair"
  • Refusing to give a number and demanding theirs first
  • Naming a range so wide it's meaningless ($120k–$200k)
  • Lowballing yourself out of fear
✗ DON'T dodge the question or say "I'll take anything."
A5

LOGISTICS

Start date, location, work authorization, hybrid/remote

20–40s
Really asking: Are you legally eligible? Can you start soon? Will location or work model be a blocker?
ELIGIBLE LOGISTICS

What they are really screening for:

No logistical blockers. They want to confirm you are authorized to work in Canada (or the posted location), can start within a reasonable window (2–4 weeks is typical), and are comfortable with the work model (remote, hybrid, onsite). Any red flags here kill the process.

I'm based in Vancouver, authorized to work in Canada, and available to start in your notice period or availability—e.g., "two weeks," "early April," "immediately". I'm comfortable with remote or hybrid, depending on what the team needs.
SPOKEN DRAFT (click to expand)

I'm based in Vancouver, authorized to work in Canada, and available to start in your notice period or availability.

I'm comfortable with remote or hybrid work—I've been your current work model—e.g., "fully remote for the last three years," "hybrid with two days onsite," "onsite five days", so I can adapt to whatever Acme's team structure requires.

No visa sponsorship needed, no relocation required, and I can commit to any travel expectations from the job description, if applicable.

Pitfalls:

  • Being vague about work authorization ("I think so")
  • Saying you can start immediately when you need to give notice
  • Negotiating work model before you have an offer
  • Oversharing personal constraints ("I need Fridays off for…")
✗ DON'T introduce new location or visa complications at this stage.

BIGGEST FAILURE

Tell me about your biggest failure, or a time the plan broke

90s–2m
Really asking: Do you own mistakes? Do you learn? Can you recover from failure?
LEARNING AGILITY OWNERSHIP STORY: AI Features / Highspot (seed)

Competency this is coded against:

Learning agility and ownership. Interviewers score whether you take responsibility for the failure (not blaming others), whether you learned a concrete lesson, and whether you changed your behavior afterward. A rubric checks: Was the failure real? Did you own it? What did you learn? Did you apply the lesson?

STRONG (4/4)
  • Named a real failure with consequences
  • Owned your part without blaming
  • Extracted a specific lesson
  • Showed behavior change in a later project
WEAK (1/4)
  • No actual failure ("we succeeded anyway")
  • Blamed others or external factors
  • Vague lesson ("work harder," "communicate more")
  • No evidence you changed

Green flags / Red flags:

  • Owned the mistake clearly
  • Named a specific lesson with an example of applying it
  • Showed humility and self-awareness
  • Blamed the PM, manager, or "the business"
  • Story ends in success (not a failure)
  • No behavior change

CONFLICT WITH COWORKER

Tell me about a conflict with a coworker

90s–2m
Really asking: Can you handle interpersonal friction? Do you escalate appropriately? Do you hold grudges?
COLLABORATION CONFLICT RESOLUTION STORY: 8 yrs full-stack (seed)

Competency this is coded against:

Collaboration and conflict resolution. Interviewers look for evidence that you engaged directly with the person, listened to their perspective, found common ground, and resolved the issue without drama or escalation to a manager unless necessary. A rubric checks: Was there real disagreement? Did you stay professional? Did you resolve it or escalate appropriately?

STRONG (4/4)
  • Named a real disagreement with stakes
  • Listened to the other person's perspective
  • Found compromise or escalated with both sides aligned
  • Maintained the working relationship
WEAK (1/4)
  • No real conflict ("we disagreed but worked it out instantly")
  • Bad-mouthed the coworker or made it personal
  • You were right, they were wrong, end of story
  • Passive-aggressive or avoided the conversation

Green flags / Red flags:

  • Engaged directly, not through a manager
  • Acknowledged the other person's valid concerns
  • Reached a resolution both could live with
  • "There was no conflict" or "I avoid conflict"
  • Blamed the other person entirely
  • Passive-aggressive or held a grudge

A rubric checks: Did you disagree constructively? Did you have a good reason? Did you commit afterward?

STRONG (4/4)

  • Undermined the decision after it was made

Green flags / Red flags:

  • Pushed back with data or a clear tradeoff
  • Asked for the manager's reasoning
  • Committed publicly once the call was made
  • "I never disagree with my manager"
  • Passive-aggressive compliance
  • Undermined the decision in front of the team

B4

TOUGH DEADLINE

Tell me about a tough deadline, or a time you had to deliver through ambiguity

90s–2m
Really asking: Can you ship under pressure? Do you cut scope intelligently? Do you communicate proactively?
BIAS FOR ACTION AMBIGUITY STORY: LLM Gateway / Highspot (lead)

Competency this is coded against:

Bias for action and operating in ambiguity. Interviewers score whether you took ownership of the problem, made decisions with incomplete information, communicated tradeoffs clearly, and delivered something valuable—even if it wasn't perfect. A rubric checks: Did you act, or wait? Did you communicate the risk? Did you ship?

STRONG (4/4)
  • Named a tight deadline or ambiguous starting point
  • Made a call on scope or approach without all the answers
  • Communicated risk and tradeoffs to stakeholders
  • Delivered on time (or early) with a working solution
WEAK (1/4)
  • No real urgency or ambiguity
  • Waited for permission or perfect information
  • Missed the deadline or delivered broken work
  • No evidence of proactive communication

Green flags / Red flags:

  • Cut scope intelligently to hit the deadline
  • Flagged risks early and often
  • Delivered something that worked, even if not perfect
  • Blamed the deadline or lack of clarity
  • Waited for someone else to make the call
  • Delivered late or incomplete work without warning

Likely follow-up probe:

"What would you have done differently with more time?" — Name the tradeoff you made (e.g., skipped tests, used a simpler architecture, deferred a feature) and how you would have improved it. Use confirmed facts from the LLM gateway project.

LEADERSHIP OWNERSHIP STORY: LLM Gateway / Highspot (lead)

Leadership without authority and ownership. STRONG (4/4)

  • Influenced peers, PMs, or managers without authority
  • Delivered a result with measurable impact
  • Showed pride in the craft or outcome
WEAK (1/4)
  • Did what you were told (not leadership)
  • Vague "we" with no clear ownership
  • No result, or result was someone else's
  • Story is just about working hard

Green flags / Red flags:

  • Identified the problem yourself
  • Convinced others to support it
  • Named a metric or outcome that shows impact
  • Vague "we did this" with no "I"
  • No clear result or just effort
  • Took credit for a team's work
