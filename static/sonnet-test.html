Part A — Top 5 HR / Recruiter Screen Questions

First 30-45 minutes · Logistics, fit, and motivation

A1. Tell me about yourself / walk me through your background

What they are really screening for: Can you tell a coherent career story in 60-90 seconds? Does your trajectory make sense for this senior full-stack role? Are you articulate and focused?

Tailored answer

The 90-second story: React/TypeScript specialist with 8 years building production systems, most recently AI-powered enterprise tools.

I'm a senior full-stack engineer with eight years of experience in React, TypeScript, and Node. I've spent my career building production systems that solve real business problems. Most recently at Highspot, from 2023 to early 2026, I designed and built an internal LLM gateway and shipped AI features that the product team relied on. Before that, at Article, I built a Figma-to-code pipeline and delivered over ten internal apps that streamlined operations. And at Appnovation, I built a COVID tracking system that scaled to four provinces. I love the full-stack challenge of turning ambiguous requirements into tools people actually use. I'm based in Vancouver, fully authorized to work in Canada, and looking for a senior full-stack role where I can keep shipping high-impact features.

Pitfalls

  • Starting with childhood, college, or irrelevant background.
  • Running over two minutes or listing every technology you've ever touched.
  • Skipping your current role or burying the most relevant work.
  • Ending without a clear "why I'm here" statement.

A2. Why are you leaving, or why are you looking now?

What they are really screening for: Is there a red flag (conflict, performance issue, job-hopping)? Are you running away from something or running toward something? Will you speak professionally about past employers?

Tailored answer

Natural timing: delivered the LLM gateway, ready for a new full-stack challenge with broader product scope.

I had a great run at Highspot. I joined to build AI features, and I delivered the internal LLM gateway that became core infrastructure for the team. By early 2026, that system was stable and in production. I'm proud of what we shipped. Now I'm looking for the next chapter—ideally a senior full-stack role where I can work on customer-facing features and contribute across the stack at a company that's growing. Highspot was a great place to work; I'm just ready for a new challenge with broader product ownership.

Pitfalls

  • Badmouthing your manager, team, or company culture.
  • Being vague or evasive—it raises suspicion.
  • Talking only about what you're leaving, not what you're seeking.
  • Oversharing about compensation, burnout, or interpersonal drama.

A3. Why this company / why this role?

What they are really screening for: Did you do your homework? Is this a mass-apply, or do you actually care about this company and role? Will you be motivated if we hire you?

Tailored answer

Structure: company mission + role fit + career growth.

ADD DETAIL: research Acme Corp's product, mission, recent funding/growth, tech stack, or team culture; replace the bracketed sections below

I'm drawn to Acme Corp because [specific reason: mission, product impact, growth stage, or technical challenge]. From what I've read, [mention something concrete: a blog post, product feature, company value, or engineering culture note]. That resonates with me. This senior full-stack role is a great fit because I've spent the last few years doing exactly this—building production systems with React, TypeScript, and Node, shipping AI features, and working across the stack. I want to join a team where I can contribute from day one and grow into deeper ownership. This feels like the right place.

Pitfalls

  • Generic answers that could apply to any company ("you have a great culture").
  • Focusing only on what you'll learn, not what you'll contribute.
  • Admitting you know nothing about the company or role.
  • Overpraising in a way that feels insincere or researched from a script.

A4. What are your salary expectations?

What they are really screening for: Are you within budget? Are your expectations realistic for the level? Will you be difficult to close if we move forward?

Tailored answer

Anchor on market, signal flexibility, and defer to their range if possible.

ADD DETAIL: your target base salary range and currency (e.g., "$140K–$160K CAD" or "$110K–$130K USD"), plus whether total comp (equity, bonus) matters to you

Based on my research for senior full-stack roles in [location/market], I'm looking in the range of [your range]. That said, I'm flexible depending on the total compensation package—equity, bonus, benefits—and the growth opportunity. I'm more focused on finding the right team and role than optimizing the last few thousand dollars. Do you have a range in mind for this role?

Pitfalls

  • Giving a single number instead of a range—it limits negotiation room.
  • Saying "I'm open to anything" or refusing to name a number—you lose anchoring power.
  • Anchoring too high or too low without market research.
  • Forgetting to mention currency (CAD vs USD matters).

A5. Logistics: availability, start date, work authorization, location, remote/hybrid/onsite

What they are really screening for: Can you start in a reasonable timeframe? Are there visa, location, or work-mode dealbreakers? Will onboarding be complicated?

Tailored answer

Green lights across the board: authorized, Vancouver-based, flexible on timing and work mode.

I'm based in Vancouver and fully authorized to work in Canada—no visa sponsorship needed. I'm available to start within two to three weeks, or I can align with your onboarding schedule if you need a bit more time. I'm flexible on work mode—I've worked remotely, hybrid, and onsite, and I'm comfortable with whatever works best for the team. No constraints on my end.

Pitfalls

  • Being vague about work authorization or leaving them guessing about visa needs.
  • Saying you need three months to start without explanation—it raises flags.
  • Being rigid about remote work if the role is hybrid or onsite (or vice versa).
  • Oversharing about personal constraints (childcare, relocation drama, etc.).

Part B — Top 5 Behavioral Questions

First behavioral round · Competency-coded STAR stories

B1. Tell me about your biggest failure (or a time the plan broke)

Competency this is coded against: Self-awareness, learning agility, resilience, ownership, candor.

What a strong (4/4) answer looks like

  • Real failure with stakes (missed deadline, production bug, bad decision), not a humble-brag.
  • Owns the mistake without deflecting blame to others or circumstances.
  • Specific corrective action and a concrete lesson applied to later work.
  • Demonstrates resilience: recovered, rebuilt trust, improved process.

What a weak (1/4) answer looks like

  • Failure is trivial, ancient (college), or a disguised success ("I worked too hard").
  • Blames others, bad luck, or vague "communication issues."
  • No concrete lesson or change in behavior afterward.
  • Defensive tone or avoids naming a real failure.

Green flags / red flags

  • Green: Vulnerability, specific corrective action, evidence of learning, rebuilt trust.
  • Red: Blame-shifting, vagueness, no concrete lesson, refusal to name a failure.

STAR answer

ADD DETAIL: no confirmed failure story in the input—identify a project that shipped late, a technical decision you reversed, an architecture you had to rebuild, or a feature that didn't work as planned; add facts before using this in an interview

Potential threads from resume to explore:

  • LLM gateway at Highspot—any false starts, performance issues, or design mistakes?
  • Figma-to-code pipeline at Article—did the first version work, or did you have to pivot?
  • COVID tracking system at Appnovation—tight timeline, any missed requirements or launch bugs?

Once you have the facts, structure it like this:

SITUATION
[Context: project, team, timeline, what was at stake]
TASK
[Your responsibility, the goal, what you were trying to achieve]
ACTION
[What you did wrong, the mistake, the moment you realized it, what you did to recover]
RESULT
[Outcome, what you learned, how you changed your approach, evidence you applied the lesson later]

Likely follow-up probe

"What would you do differently if you faced that situation today?"

Don't pick a story where someone else failed and you heroically saved it—that's not a failure story, it's a leadership humble-brag.

B2. Tell me about a conflict with a coworker

Competency this is coded against: Collaboration, empathy, conflict resolution, communication, professionalism.

What a strong (4/4) answer looks like

  • Real disagreement with stakes (not a mild difference of opinion).
  • Shows empathy: understood the other person's perspective.
  • Took proactive steps to resolve it (1:1 conversation, compromise, escalation if needed).
  • Positive outcome: working relationship improved, project moved forward.

What a weak (1/4) answer looks like

  • No real conflict, or trivializes it ("we disagreed on a variable name").
  • Paints the other person as incompetent or irrational.
  • Avoids the conflict or lets it fester without resolution.
  • No reflection on their own role in the disagreement.

Green flags / red flags

  • Green: Empathy, direct communication, win-win outcome, maintained relationship.
  • Red: Blaming, avoidance, unresolved conflict, describes coworker as "difficult" without self-reflection.

STAR answer

ADD DETAIL: no confirmed conflict story in the input—identify a disagreement over architecture, timeline pressure with a PM, design conflict with a designer, or cross-team dependency issue; add facts before using this in an interview

Potential threads from resume to explore:

  • LLM gateway—any friction with product, security, or infra teams over design or scope?
  • Figma-to-code pipeline—disagreement with designers or PMs about requirements or feasibility?
  • COVID tracking system—tight timeline with four provinces; any stakeholder or team conflicts?

Once you have the facts, structure it like this:

SITUATION
[Project context, who you were working with, what triggered the conflict]
TASK
[What you needed to deliver, why the disagreement mattered]
ACTION
[How you approached the conversation, what you proposed, how you listened, any compromise]
RESULT
[Resolution, impact on the project, what happened to the relationship afterward]

Likely follow-up probe

"How did you ensure that person felt heard, even though you disagreed?"

Don't describe the other person as unreasonable or stupid. If you can't tell the story without making them the villain, pick a different story.

B3. Tell me about a time you disagreed with a manager or pushed back

Competency this is coded against: Healthy dissent, conviction, communication, risk management, knowing when to disagree and commit.

What a strong (4/4) answer looks like

  • Real disagreement on something important (technical direction, timeline, scope).
  • Pushed back respectfully with data, reasoning, or risk analysis.
  • Manager either agreed and changed course, or you disagreed-and-committed professionally.
  • Shows you can hold a position without being rigid or insubordinate.

What a weak (1/4) answer looks like

  • Trivial disagreement or no real stakes.
  • Pushed back emotionally or without data.
  • Undermined the manager or went around them.
  • Can't name a time they disagreed—signals they're a yes-person.

Green flags / red flags

  • Green: Respectful dissent, data-driven argument, disagree-and-commit if overruled, good outcome either way.
  • Red: Describes manager as incompetent, insubordination, can't name a disagreement, or held a grudge.

STAR answer

ADD DETAIL: no confirmed pushback story in the input—identify a time you advocated for a different technical approach, timeline, scope cut, or design decision against initial direction; add facts before using this in an interview

Potential threads from resume to explore:

  • LLM gateway—did you push back on scope, tech stack, security requirements, or timeline?
  • Figma-to-code pipeline—any debate over architecture, tooling, or how to build it?
  • COVID tracking system—did you advocate for cutting scope, changing tech, or adjusting the timeline?

Once you have the facts, structure it like this:

SITUATION
[Project context, manager's initial direction, why it mattered]
TASK
[Your concern, what was at risk if you didn't push back]
ACTION
[How you raised it, the data or reasoning you used, how you framed it]
RESULT
[Manager's decision, what happened, impact on the project, what you learned]

Likely follow-up probe

"What did you do after your manager decided to move forward with their original plan?"

Don't describe your manager as clueless or make it sound like you went around them. The best version of this story is one where you either changed their mind with data, or you disagreed and committed professionally.

B4. Tell me about a tough deadline or delivering through ambiguity

Competency this is coded against: Execution, adaptability, prioritization, ownership, working through uncertainty.

What a strong (4/4) answer looks like

  • Real time pressure or unclear requirements, not a routine sprint.
  • Shows how you clarified scope, prioritized ruthlessly, or made smart tradeoffs.
  • Delivered something valuable on time, even if imperfect.
  • Evidence of communication: kept stakeholders aligned despite the chaos.

What a weak (1/4) answer looks like

  • Story is routine work or normal sprint pressure.
  • Hero narrative: "I stayed up all night"—no mention of prioritization or tradeoffs.
  • Missed the deadline with no explanation of what you learned.
  • Blames ambiguity instead of showing how you navigated it.

Green flags / red flags

  • Green: Clear prioritization, smart tradeoffs, stakeholder communication, delivered value despite constraints.
  • Red: Pure hero hours, no prioritization, missed deadline without learning, blames others for ambiguity.

STAR answer

COVID tracking system: tight timeline, four provinces, ambiguous requirements—shipped on time by clarifying must-haves and cutting scope smart.
SITUATION
At Appnovation, I was tasked with building a COVID tracking system that would be used by four provinces. The timeline was tight—ADD DETAIL: how tight? weeks? months?—and the requirements were still shifting because public health policies were evolving in real time.
TASK
I needed to build a system that could track cases, testing, and reporting across four different jurisdictions, each with slightly different data formats and reporting needs, and get it into production before ADD DETAIL: deadline or event that drove urgency.
ACTION
First, I set up a call with stakeholders from each province to identify the must-have features versus nice-to-haves. We agreed on a core set of fields and reporting views that worked for everyone. I prioritized building the data ingestion and core dashboard first, and punted the custom provincial views to phase two. I also set up a lightweight staging environment so stakeholders could validate data as I built, which caught mismatches early. ADD DETAIL: any specific tech decisions or tradeoffs? any team collaboration?
RESULT
We shipped the core system on time, and all four provinces were able to use it for ADD DETAIL: what did they use it for? case tracking? reporting to the public?. The phase-two custom views rolled out two weeks later. The key lesson was: when requirements are ambiguous, clarify the core outcome early and ruthlessly cut scope to protect the deadline. That approach has worked for me ever since.

Likely follow-up probe

"What would you have done differently if you had one more week?"

Don't make it a hero story about working weekends. The interviewer wants to know you can prioritize and ship smart, not that you can grind.

B5. Tell me about your proudest achievement or leading without authority

Competency this is coded against: Impact, initiative, influence, leadership, ownership beyond the job description.

What a strong (4/4) answer looks like

  • High-impact outcome with measurable results or broad adoption.
  • Shows initiative: you drove it, not just executed someone else's plan.
  • If "leading without authority," demonstrates influence, coalition-building, or earned trust.
  • Story reveals what you care about and how you define success.

What a weak (1/4) answer looks like

  • Routine work or small bug fix framed as an achievement.
  • Can't articulate the impact or why it mattered.
  • Takes sole credit for a team effort without acknowledging collaborators.
  • Story is ancient (college project) or not relevant to this role.

Green flags / red flags

  • Green: Clear impact, initiative, collaboration, humility, passion for the work.
  • Red: Vague impact, credit-hogging, trivial scope, or story doesn't connect to role.

STAR answer

LLM gateway at Highspot: designed and built the internal infrastructure that powered AI features for the product team—became core infra.
SITUATION
At Highspot, the product team wanted to ship AI-powered features, but we didn't have a safe, scalable way to integrate LLMs. Each team was considering building their own one-off integrations, which would have been a security and cost nightmare.
TASK
I was asked to design and build an internal LLM gateway that the product team could use as shared infrastructure. It needed to handle authentication, rate limiting, cost tracking, and multiple LLM providers. ADD DETAIL: timeline? team size? specific LLM providers or requirements?
ACTION
I started by talking to the product and security teams to understand their requirements. I designed the gateway with a plugin architecture so we could swap providers without breaking downstream services. I built the core in Node and TypeScript, added observability hooks, and wrote documentation so other engineers could integrate without needing my help. ADD DETAIL: any technical challenges or design decisions worth highlighting? I also ran a demo for the engineering org to get feedback before we rolled it out.
RESULT
The gateway went into production and became the standard way to use LLMs at Highspot. ADD DETAIL: how many teams or features used it? any cost or performance wins? user feedback? I'm proud of it because it unblocked the product team, reduced security risk, and became core infrastructure. It's the kind of high-leverage work I want to keep doing—building tools that multiply the impact of other engineers.

Likely follow-up probe

"What was the hardest technical or organizational challenge you faced while building that?"

Don't just describe what you built—explain why it mattered, who it helped, and what the measurable impact was. Impact is the difference between a good story and a great one.

Questions to Ask Them

Smart questions that show you care and help you evaluate fit

  • → What does success look like for this role in the first six months?
    Reveals priorities, expectations, and how they measure impact.
  • → What's the biggest technical or organizational challenge the team is facing right now?
    Shows you're thinking about real problems, not just the job description.
  • → How do engineers here grow into senior+ or staff roles?
    Signals career ambition and helps you assess growth paths.
  • → What's something you wish you knew before joining this team or company?
    Personal, disarming question that often reveals honest insights.
  • → How does the team balance shipping fast with maintaining quality or paying down tech debt?
    Reveals engineering culture, priorities, and how they handle tradeoffs.
