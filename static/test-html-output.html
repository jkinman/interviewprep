Part A — Top 5 Recruiter Screen Questions

A1. Tell me about yourself / Walk me through your background

What they are really screening for

Can you tell a coherent, relevant story in 60–90 seconds? Do your experience and level match the role? Are you a clear communicator? This is not "tell me your life story"—it's "prove you can do this job and that you're senior."

Tailored Answer

"I'm a senior full-stack engineer with eight years building production React, TypeScript, and Node.js systems."

Right now I'm at Highspot, where I've been since 2023. I built an LLM gateway that routes requests across multiple models and I've shipped AI features into the product. Before that I was at Article for about a year and a half. I built a Figma-to-code pipeline that let designers generate production components, and I owned more than ten internal apps. Earlier, at Appnovation, I built a COVID tracking system that ran across four provinces in Canada—high stakes, tight deadlines, real impact.

I'm looking for a senior full-stack role where I can keep building at scale, work with modern AI tooling, and have autonomy over architecture decisions. That's why I'm here.

Pitfalls

  • Going longer than 90 seconds. Recruiters lose focus. Practice with a timer.
  • Listing every technology you've touched. This is not your LinkedIn skills section.
  • Starting with "I was born in…" or college. Start with your level and current/most recent role.
  • Ending without a "why I'm here" sentence. Always close with motivation or what you're looking for.

A2. Why are you leaving, or why are you looking now?

What they are really screening for

Are you a flight risk? Are you running from a problem? Is this a thoughtful decision or desperation? Are you going to bad-mouth your current employer (red flag for culture fit)?

Tailored Answer

"I've been at Highspot since 2023 and I've done good work there—shipping the LLM gateway and AI features has been rewarding."

[ADD DETAIL: the real reason you're looking—examples: team restructure, want to work at a smaller/larger company, want to work on X problem domain, Highspot's AI roadmap shifted, compensation/equity timing, remote policy change, or you've accomplished what you set out to do and want a new challenge. Pick the true reason and phrase it neutrally.]

I'm not in a rush, but I'm ready for the right opportunity.

Pitfalls

  • Bad-mouthing Highspot, your manager, or the team. Instant red flag. Stay neutral or positive.
  • "I'm not being challenged" without evidence. Sounds like you're coasting or bored easily.
  • Being vague or evasive. "Just looking around" suggests you'll leave the new place in six months.
  • Saying you were let go without context. If that's true, say "layoff" or "org change," give one sentence of context, then pivot to what you want next.

What they are really screening for

Did you do any research, or are you mass-applying? Do your motivations align with what this company actually does? Will you accept an offer if they make one, or are you just shopping?

Tailored Answer

[ADD DETAIL: Company name] is interesting to me for a few reasons.

[ADD DETAIL: One sentence about the company's product, mission, or market position that genuinely interests you—examples: "You're solving X problem in Y industry," "I've used your product and I love how you handle Z," "You're at the stage where architecture decisions have huge leverage."]

[ADD DETAIL: One sentence about the role itself—examples: "This role owns the full stack for [team/product area], which is exactly the scope I want," "I'd get to work with [technology or problem domain] at scale," "The job description mentioned [specific responsibility]—that's work I've done and want to do more of."]

[ADD DETAIL: Optional—one sentence about team, culture, or growth if you learned something specific in research: "I saw on your eng blog that the team uses [X]," "Your VP of Eng talked about [Y] in an interview," or "A friend who works here told me [Z]."]

It feels like a place where I can have impact and keep learning.

Pitfalls

  • Generic praise that could apply to any company. "You have a great culture" or "You're a leader in tech" means nothing.
  • Making it all about you. "I want to learn React" is not a reason to join; "You're using React to solve [problem]" is.
  • Getting the company's product or business model wrong. Do five minutes of research. Check the website, LinkedIn, Glassdoor, eng blog.
  • Saying "I just need a job." Even if true, don't say it. Find one real reason you're interested.

A4. What are your salary expectations?

What they are really screening for

Are you in range for this role and level, or will we waste everyone's time? Can you have a professional negotiation, or will this get awkward? In some jurisdictions (including parts of Canada and many US states), the company may be required to share the range first—if so, they will. If they ask you first, they're trying to anchor or screen you out early.

Tailored Answer

"I'm looking for a total compensation package in the range of [ADD DETAIL: your target range, e.g., $140,000–$160,000 CAD, or $150,000–$175,000 USD—specify currency and whether this is base or total comp]."

That's based on my experience level, the scope of senior full-stack roles in [ADD DETAIL: your market—Vancouver, Toronto, remote North America, etc.], and what I've seen for similar roles. I'm flexible depending on the full package—equity, benefits, growth opportunity—but that's the range I'm targeting. [ADD DETAIL: If you have a competing offer or a current salary you're trying to match or beat, mention it here: "I'm currently at X, and I'm looking to move up with this next role."]

Can you share the range you've budgeted for this role?

Pitfalls

  • "I'm flexible" or "whatever you think is fair." This kills your negotiating power. Give a number or range.
  • Asking for a number wildly out of market. Do research. Use Levels.fyi, Glassdoor, Blind, or the jurisdiction's pay transparency data.
  • Refusing to give a number when pressed. "I'd prefer not to say" can work once, but if they push, give a range or you'll be screened out.
  • Confusing base and total comp. Be clear whether your number includes equity, bonus, etc. In Canada, most offers are stated as base; in US Big Tech, "total comp" is common.
  • Sharing your current salary unprompted. In many places they can't ask. If you share, it anchors you low. Only share if it helps your negotiation ("I'm currently at X, looking to move to Y").

What they are really screening for

Are there any dealbreakers? Do we need to sponsor a visa? Can you start in our timeline? Will the work model (remote, hybrid, onsite) work for you? This is a checkbox section—answer clearly and don't create unnecessary concerns.

Tailored Answer

"I'm based in Vancouver and I'm authorized to work in Canada without sponsorship."

[ADD DETAIL: Your availability—examples: "I have a notice period of two weeks, so I could start in mid-[month]," "I'm currently employed and would give two weeks' notice," "I'm available to start immediately," or "I'm finishing a project and would want to start after [date]."]

[ADD DETAIL: Your location and work model preference—examples: "I'm open to remote, hybrid, or onsite in Vancouver," "I'm looking for fully remote roles," "I can work hybrid if the office is in Vancouver or the lower mainland," or "I'm open to relocating to [city] for the right role."]

No other constraints on my end.

Pitfalls

  • Being vague about work authorization. If you need sponsorship, say so clearly. If you don't, say "I'm authorized to work in Canada" (or wherever) so they don't have to ask.
  • Saying you can start immediately when you're currently employed. It signals you'll leave the new job just as abruptly. Two weeks' notice is standard and expected.
  • Surprising them with a constraint late in the process. If you can only work remotely, or you can't relocate, or you need to start after a specific date, say so now.
  • Over-explaining personal reasons. "I need to start after my wedding" is fine in one sentence; a five-minute story is not.

Part B — Top 5 Behavioral Questions

Recruiters often ask 1–2 behavioral questions in a 30-minute screen. The full set below is more common in a later round with a hiring manager or a panel. Prepare all five so you're ready for any format.

B1. Tell me about your biggest failure (or a time the plan broke)

Competency this is coded against

Learning agility, ownership, resilience. Interviewers use this to see: Do you take responsibility for mistakes, or do you blame others? Do you learn and change behavior, or repeat the same error? Can you talk about failure without getting defensive? A strong answer shows you owned the problem, identified the root cause, and applied the lesson later. A weak answer deflects blame ("my manager didn't give me enough time") or offers no real failure ("I worked too hard").

What a strong (4/4) answer looks like

  • A real failure where you owned part of the outcome (a missed deadline, a technical decision that backfired, a miscommunication that caused a problem).
  • Clear description of what went wrong and your role in it—not "the team failed," but "I failed to [X]."
  • Concrete actions you took to fix it or mitigate the damage.
  • A specific lesson you learned and evidence you applied it in a later situation (this is the gold standard for a 4/4).

What a weak (1/4) answer looks like

  • "I've never really failed" or "I can't think of one"—this is disqualifying.
  • A story where the failure was entirely someone else's fault and you were the hero who fixed it.
  • A humble-brag ("I work too hard," "I care too much").
  • No learning, no behavior change, or a vague lesson like "communication is important."

Green flags / Red flags

Green Flags
  • Uses "I" not "we" when describing the mistake
  • Names the lesson in one clear sentence
  • Shows up later: "So in my next project, I did [X] differently"
  • Appropriate scope: a real setback, but not a catastrophic company-ending disaster
Red Flags
  • Blames the manager, the PM, the designer, or "the business"
  • No ownership: "I was told to do it this way"
  • No result or resolution—just "it was bad, then we moved on"
  • The "failure" is actually a success story

B2. Tell me about a conflict with a coworker

Competency this is coded against

Collaboration, conflict resolution, emotional intelligence. Interviewers want to know: Can you disagree professionally? Do you escalate appropriately, or do you let conflicts fester? Do you respect other disciplines (design, PM, QA)? A strong answer shows you addressed the conflict directly, found a resolution, and maintained the relationship. A weak answer bad-mouths the other person or pretends conflict never happens.

What a strong (4/4) answer looks like

  • A specific disagreement about work (technical approach, priority, scope, process)—not a personality clash.
  • You initiated a conversation, listened to the other person's perspective, and found a path forward (compromise, escalation to a manager for a decision, or data-driven resolution).
  • The relationship stayed intact or improved—you still worked together effectively afterward.
  • Appropriate scope: a real disagreement, but not a screaming match or HR incident.

What a weak (1/4) answer looks like

  • "I've never had a conflict" or "I get along with everyone"—this is not credible.
  • Describing the other person as incompetent, lazy, or malicious.
  • No resolution—just "we agreed to disagree" or "I went around them."
  • Making it about personality: "They were difficult to work with."

Green flags / Red flags

Green Flags
  • Describes the other person's perspective fairly: "They wanted X because…"
  • You took the initiative to resolve it (scheduled a 1:1, pulled in a mediator)
  • Shows empathy: "I understood why they felt that way"
  • Positive ending: "We shipped the feature and worked well together afterward"
Red Flags
  • Bad-mouths the coworker or questions their competence
  • Avoided the conflict or let it drag on
  • Escalated immediately without trying to resolve it directly
  • No accountability: "I was right, they were wrong"

B3. Tell me about a time you disagreed with a manager, or pushed back

Competency this is coded against

Judgment, courage, communication, escalation. Interviewers want to know: Will you speak up when something is wrong, or will you silently execute bad decisions? Can you disagree respectfully? Do you have a point of view, or do you just follow orders? A strong answer shows you raised a concern, made your case with data or reasoning, and respected the manager's final call. A weak answer makes you look passive or insubordinate.

What a strong (4/4) answer looks like

  • A specific decision or direction from your manager that you thought was wrong (technical approach, timeline, priority, scope).
  • You raised the concern privately, with reasoning or data, and proposed an alternative.
  • Outcome: either the manager changed their mind, or they heard you out and made the final call, and you committed to executing it ("disagree and commit").
  • Appropriate scope: a real disagreement, but not "my manager was incompetent" or "I went around them."

What a weak (1/4) answer looks like

  • "I always agreed with my manager" or "I've never had to push back"—this suggests you have no point of view.
  • Describing the manager as wrong, unreasonable, or not technical enough.
  • Pushing back publicly or escalating over your manager's head without trying to resolve it first.
  • No resolution, or you sabotaged the decision after the fact.

Green flags / Red flags

Green Flags
  • Raised the concern early and privately
  • Came with data, a prototype, or a clear alternative
  • Respected the manager's final decision even if it didn't go your way
  • Positive framing: "My manager and I had different views on…"
Red Flags
  • Bad-mouths the manager or questions their competence
  • Pushed back in a public meeting or in front of the team
  • Went around the manager to their boss without trying to resolve it first
  • Executed the decision half-heartedly or said "I told you so" when it failed

B4. Tell me about a tough deadline, or a time you had to deliver through ambiguity

Competency this is coded against

Bias for action, prioritization, ownership, execution. Interviewers want to know: Can you deliver when the requirements are unclear or the timeline is tight? Do you take ownership and figure it out, or do you wait for perfect clarity? Do you manage scope and trade-offs, or do you miss deadlines? A strong answer shows you clarified what mattered, made smart trade-offs, delivered the core functionality, and communicated risk. A weak answer says "I worked 80 hours" with no prioritization or strategy.

What a strong (4/4) answer looks like

  • A specific project with a hard deadline (launch date, customer commitment, regulatory requirement) or unclear requirements.
  • You took ownership: clarified the must-haves, cut scope, broke the work into phases, or brought in help.
  • You delivered the core functionality on time (or early), and you communicated risk or trade-offs to stakeholders.
  • Appropriate scope for senior: you led the execution, not just "I stayed late."

What a weak (1/4) answer looks like

  • No specific deadline or no real ambiguity—just "it was busy."
  • Hero story: "I worked nights and weekends and saved the project." This signals poor planning or boundary issues.
  • No prioritization or trade-offs—just "I did everything."
  • Missed the deadline, or delivered something that didn't work.

Green flags / Red flags

Green Flags
  • Named the must-haves and cut or deferred the rest
  • Communicated risk early: "I told the PM we could do A and B, but not C"
  • Delivered on time and followed up on the deferred work
  • Reasonable effort: "I put in some extra hours" is fine; "I didn't sleep for a week" is a red flag
Red Flags
  • No trade-offs or prioritization—just brute force
  • Didn't communicate risk, then surprised stakeholders at the deadline
  • Blames the deadline or the stakeholders for being unreasonable
  • Missed the deadline and doesn't own it

STAR Answer

Situation

At Appnovation, I was building a COVID tracking system that four provinces in Canada were going to use. The timeline was tight—we had about six weeks from kickoff to launch. The stakes were high. This was early pandemic, and provinces needed a tool to track cases, exposures, and testing.

Task

I owned the full-stack build—React front end, Node.js backend, database design, and integration with provincial health data sources. The challenge was that the requirements kept changing because the provinces didn't fully know what they needed yet. Each province had slightly different reporting rules.

Action

I ran a prioritization session with the PMs and the provincial contacts in the first week. I asked: what's the minimum viable version that lets you go live? We agreed on a core feature set—case entry, contact tracing workflows, and a basic dashboard. I pushed the province-specific customizations to phase two. I built the core in three weeks, then spent the last three weeks on testing and the highest-priority customizations. I communicated weekly: here's what's done, here's what's at risk, here's what we're deferring.

Result

We launched on time. All four provinces were live within the six-week window. The phase-two features shipped two weeks later. The system handled thousands of case entries per day without issues. I learned that tight deadlines are manageable if you prioritize ruthlessly and communicate risk early. I've used that approach on every tight-deadline project since.

B5. Tell me about your proudest achievement, or a time you led without formal authority

Competency this is coded against

Leadership, influence, impact, initiative. Interviewers want to know: What do you care about? What does "good work" look like to you? Can you lead a project or influence a team without being a manager? Do you take ownership beyond your immediate task? A strong answer shows you identified a need, took initiative, brought others along, and delivered a meaningful result. A weak answer is a routine task dressed up as an achievement.

What a strong (4/4) answer looks like

  • A project you initiated or a problem you solved that had real impact (users, revenue, team efficiency, product quality).
  • You didn't wait for permission—you saw the need, made the case, and took ownership.
  • You influenced or coordinated others (cross-functional partners, other engineers, leadership) without being their manager.
  • Quantified impact where possible (users, time saved, incidents prevented, etc.).

What a weak (1/4) answer looks like

  • A routine project you were assigned—no initiative or ownership beyond your job.
  • Vague impact: "It was really important to the team" with no specifics.
  • No leadership or influence—just "I did my part."
  • Overclaiming: "I saved the company" when you built a small tool.

Green flags / Red flags

Green Flags
  • You saw a gap and filled it: "No one was working on X, so I proposed…"
  • You brought others along: "I got buy-in from the PM and design"
  • You quantified the impact: "10 internal apps," "4 provinces," "reduced latency by X"
  • Appropriate pride: you're genuinely proud, but not arrogant
Red Flags
  • Takes full credit when it was clearly a team effort
  • No clear impact or outcome
  • The achievement is just doing your job
  • Sounds rehearsed or like you're reading a resume bullet
