# Part A — Top 5 HR / recruiter screen questions

---

#### A1. Tell me about yourself / walk me through your background

**What they are really screening for:**  
Can you communicate clearly and position your experience at the right level? Are you a senior full-stack generalist with modern stack fluency, or a narrow specialist? Does your story arc make sense for the role you're applying to?

**Tailored answer:**

> "I'm a senior full-stack engineer with eight years building production systems in React, TypeScript, and Node. Most recently at Highspot, I built an LLM gateway and shipped AI features into the product. Before that at Article, I led a Figma-to-code pipeline and built over ten internal apps that the team still uses. Earlier at Appnovation, I built a COVID tracking system that ran across four provinces during the pandemic. I'm looking for a senior IC role where I can keep shipping complex full-stack features and work on problems that touch real users. I'm based in Vancouver and authorized to work in Canada."

**Pitfalls:**
- ✕ Rambling through every job in chronological order without a through-line  
- ✕ Using jargon or acronyms the recruiter won't recognize ("we refactored the SSR hydration layer")  
- ✕ Stopping at two sentences and waiting for the next question (feels junior)  
- ✕ Ending with "so yeah, that's me" instead of a clear statement of what you want next  

---

#### A2. Why are you leaving, or why are you looking now?

**What they are really screening for:**  
Flight risk. Are you running from something (bad manager, PIP, burnout, conflict), or moving toward something? Will you stay long enough to deliver value? Is the reason aligned with what this role offers?

**Tailored answer:**

> [ADD DETAIL: your real reason — examples: Highspot ended in 2026 (layoff? contract end? team dissolved?), you want a new challenge, you want to work on X type of problem, you want a different stage company, etc.]

**Structure if you were laid off or the role ended:**  
> "Highspot [ended my role / had a reduction] in early 2026. I'm proud of what we shipped—the LLM gateway is in production—and I'm looking for a senior IC role where I can keep building full-stack features that touch users. I want to join a team that values ownership and ships quickly."

**Structure if you're still employed or left by choice:**  
> "I've been at Highspot for about three years and I've shipped the work I signed up to do—our AI features are live and stable. I'm looking for [a new technical challenge / a different problem space / a team at X stage] where I can [specific thing this new role offers]."

**Pitfalls:**
- ✕ Saying "I'm bored" or "not learning anymore" (sounds like you'll leave the next role in a year)  
- ✕ Bad-mouthing Highspot, Article, or any manager by name  
- ✕ Giving a five-minute story about org politics (recruiter will flag you as high-drama)  
- ✕ Saying "I'll take anything" or "just exploring" (signals no conviction)  

---

#### A3. Why this company / why this role?

**What they are really screening for:**  
Did you apply to 100 jobs with the same resume, or do you actually want *this* job? Will you accept the offer if we make one, or are you using us for leverage? Do you understand what we do?

**Tailored answer:**

> [ADD DETAIL: the company name, and 2–3 specific reasons — examples: "I've used [product] and I want to work on tools engineers actually rely on," "I'm excited about [technical problem the company is known for]," "I want to join a team at [stage] where I can [own features end-to-end / work directly with users / scale systems to X]," "I've heard great things about [specific team, leader, or eng culture trait]."]

**Reusable structure:**  
> "Three things. One, [specific thing about the product or mission]. Two, [specific thing about the technical challenge or stack]. Three, [specific thing about the team, stage, or company value that maps to what you want]. This role feels like a great match for where I am—senior IC, full-stack, shipping features that matter."

**Pitfalls:**
- ✕ Saying "I love the culture" with no specifics (sounds like you read the careers page once)  
- ✕ Leading with "I need visa sponsorship" or "the salary range works" (true, but not motivating)  
- ✕ Talking only about what you'll learn, not what you'll contribute  
- ✕ Generic praise that could apply to any company ("you're innovative, you move fast")  

---

#### A4. What are your salary expectations?

**What they are really screening for:**  
Are you in range, or will we waste everyone's time? Are you anchor-able, or will you fight us at offer time? Can you talk about money like a senior professional?

**Tailored answer:**

> [ADD DETAIL: your number or range, and currency (CAD or USD). Example: "I'm looking for 140 to 160K CAD base, plus equity. That's in line with senior full-stack roles in Vancouver with my experience."]

**Structure if you don't have a number yet:**  
> "I'm looking for a competitive senior IC package—base, equity, and benefits. For Vancouver and my experience level, I'd expect [range] CAD. I'm flexible if the equity and growth opportunity are strong. What's the budget for this role?"

**Pitfalls:**
- ✕ Saying "I'm flexible" or "whatever's fair" with no anchor (recruiter will lowball)  
- ✕ Giving a number way outside the posted range or market rate (instant screen-out)  
- ✕ Saying "I made X at my last job, so I need X plus 20%" (sounds like you anchor on the past, not the market)  
- ✕ Refusing to give a number and saying "what's your range?" in a hostile tone (makes negotiation harder later)  

---

#### A5. Logistics: availability, start date, work authorization, location, willingness to work the posted model

**What they are really screening for:**  
Are there any hard blockers (visa, location, notice period, remote/onsite mismatch) that would kill the process later? Can you start soon enough?

**Tailored answer:**

> "I'm in Vancouver, I'm authorized to work in Canada, and I can start in two to four weeks depending on the offer timeline. I'm open to remote, hybrid, or onsite depending on what the team does."

**Pitfalls:**
- ✕ Saying "I need four months' notice" unless you actually do (flags you as high-friction)  
- ✕ Saying "I'll relocate anywhere" if you have a family or strong location preference (you'll ghost the offer)  
- ✕ Giving a vague answer about work authorization ("I think I'm good?")—if you need sponsorship, say so now  
- ✕ Negotiating remote work in the recruiter screen if the JD says onsite (wait for the hiring manager, or walk away)

#### B1. Tell me about your biggest failure (or a time the plan broke)

**Competency this is coded against:**  
Learning agility, ownership, judgment under pressure, accountability. The interviewer is not asking if you've ever failed—everyone has—but whether you own mistakes, learn from them, and change your behavior. A strong answer shows you spotted the failure early, took responsibility (not "my team" or "we"), fixed it, and carried the lesson into the next project. Weak answers blame others, minimize the failure, or skip the learning.

**What a strong (4/4) answer looks like:**
- ◆ A real failure with consequences (missed deadline, broke production, misread the user need, made the wrong technical bet)  
- ◆ You own your part—your decision, your code, your assumption—not "the team" or "the requirements"  
- ◆ Clear actions you took to fix it and mitigate damage  
- ◆ A specific lesson and evidence you changed behavior afterward  

**What a weak (1/4) answer looks like:**
- ✕ No failure (or "I've never really failed")  
- ✕ Blaming others ("the PM gave bad requirements," "my manager didn't give me time")  
- ✕ Vague "we" with no individual accountability  
- ✕ No result or lesson, or a fake lesson ("I learned to work harder")  

**Green flags / red flags:**
- ✅ Green: "I misread the constraint," "I should have asked the question earlier," "I changed how I scope work now"  
- ✅ Green: You fixed it yourself or proposed the fix  
- ✅ Green: The lesson is specific and you used it on the next project  
- ❌ Red: "My manager didn't support me," "the customer was wrong," "I had no choice"  
- ❌ Red: The failure is trivial ("I missed a standup once")  
- ❌ Red: No learning, or the learning is "trust no one"

#### B2. Tell me about a conflict with a coworker

**Competency this is coded against:**  
Collaboration, communication, conflict resolution, empathy, professionalism. The interviewer wants to see that you can disagree without damaging relationships, that you listen to the other person's constraints, and that you find a solution instead of escalating or going silent. A strong answer shows you engaged directly (not through Slack or a third party), understood the other person's position, and reached a working resolution. Weak answers blame the coworker, say "there was no conflict," or involve HR without trying to resolve it yourself.

**What a strong (4/4) answer looks like:**
- ◆ A real disagreement with a peer (not your manager, not a user, not "the business")—engineer, PM, designer, etc.  
- ◆ You talked to them directly, in private, early in the conflict  
- ◆ You listened to their reasoning and acknowledged their constraints or perspective  
- ◆ Clear resolution: compromise, one of you deferred, or you escalated *together* to get a decision  

**What a weak (1/4) answer looks like:**
- ✕ "I've never had a conflict" (feels dishonest or unaware)  
- ✕ "My coworker was wrong and I convinced them" (no empathy, no listening)  
- ✕ You went to their manager or HR before talking to them  
- ✕ The conflict never resolved and you just worked around them  

**Green flags / red flags:**
- ✅ Green: "I scheduled a 1:1," "I asked them to explain their reasoning," "we found a middle ground"  
- ✅ Green: You owned your part of the miscommunication or misunderstanding  
- ✅ Green: The relationship stayed intact or improved  
- ❌ Red: "They were difficult," "I had to escalate because they wouldn't listen"  
- ❌ Red: The other person doesn't get to speak in the story  
- ❌ Red: You won and they lost, with no compromise

#### B3. Tell me about a time you disagreed with a manager, or pushed back

**Competency this is coded against:**  
Judgment, courage, communication, influence without authority, knowing when to defer. The interviewer wants evidence that you can respectfully challenge a decision when you have new information or a better idea, that you bring data or reasoning instead of just saying "I don't want to," and that you can disagree and commit if the manager decides to go the other way. A strong answer shows you pushed back once, with context, listened to their response, and either convinced them or committed to their call. Weak answers trash the manager, say "I never disagreed" (feels untrustworthy), or show you pushed back repeatedly and ignored their decision.

**What a strong (4/4) answer looks like:**
- ◆ A real disagreement on a decision, priority, or technical choice  
- ◆ You raised it in private, with reasoning and data, not in a public meeting or Slack rant  
- ◆ You listened to their constraints (deadline, budget, strategy you didn't have visibility into)  
- ◆ Clear outcome: they changed their mind, or you committed to their decision and executed well  

**What a weak (1/4) answer looks like:**
- ✕ "I never disagreed" or "I always trust my manager" (sounds junior or fake)  
- ✕ Bad-mouthing the manager ("they didn't understand the tech")  
- ✕ You pushed back many times and they "refused to listen" (you look stubborn)  
- ✕ No resolution—you disagreed, then quietly did it your way anyway  

**Green flags / red flags:**
- ✅ Green: "I scheduled a 1:1," "I brought data," "I asked what constraint I was missing"  
- ✅ Green: You committed to their decision even if you disagreed  
- ✅ Green: The relationship stayed strong  
- ❌ Red: "My manager was wrong and I proved it"  
- ❌ Red: You escalated over their head to their manager  
- ❌ Red: You "agreed" in the meeting, then built it your way

#### B4. Tell me about a tough deadline, or a time you had to deliver through ambiguity

**Competency this is coded against:**  
Bias for action, ownership, prioritization, judgment, communication under pressure. The interviewer wants to see that you can ship when the requirements are unclear, the timeline is tight, or the scope keeps changing—that you don't freeze or wait for perfect information. A strong answer shows you clarified the must-haves, made a call on what to cut or defer, communicated the trade-offs, and delivered something valuable. Weak answers blame others for the ambiguity, say "we missed the deadline," or show you built everything and burned out the team.

**What a strong (4/4) answer looks like:**
- ◆ A real tight deadline or unclear scope (launch, demo, customer ask, incident)  
- ◆ You drove clarity—asked the right questions, defined the MVP, made trade-off calls  
- ◆ You shipped on time (or early), with appropriate scope  
- ◆ You communicated risk and status clearly to stakeholders  

**What a weak (1/4) answer looks like:**
- ✕ You missed the deadline and blame the PM, the requirements, or "the business"

#### B5. Tell me about your proudest achievement, or a time you led without formal authority

**Competency this is coded against:**  
Leadership, ownership, influence, impact, initiative. The interviewer wants evidence that you can drive a project end-to-end, rally a team, and deliver something valuable—without needing a VP title or a mandate. A strong answer shows you identified the opportunity, convinced others it mattered, coordinated the work, and shipped a result that the company or users cared about. Weak answers claim credit for a team effort ("we shipped X"), are too small-scope for the level (a bug fix), or are vague about your individual contribution.

**What a strong (4/4) answer looks like:**
- ◆ You drove the project—identified the need, made the case, got buy-in, coordinated people  
- ◆ Clear individual contribution, even if others helped  
- ◆ Meaningful result with evidence (users adopted it, it's still running, it unblocked the team, it saved time or money)  
- ◆ Appropriate scope for a senior IC (not a VP-level reorg, not a junior bug fix)  

**What a weak (1/4) answer looks like:**
- ✕ All "we," no "I"—interviewer can't see your contribution  
- ✕ No result, or the result is "we learned a lot"  
- ✕ You were told to do it (that's your job, not leadership)  
- ✕ The story is too small ("I refactored a file") or too big ("I saved the company")  

**Green flags / red flags:**
- ✅ Green: "I proposed it," "I pulled together [people] and made the case," "I owned the execution"  
- ✅ Green: Clear before/after (slow → fast, manual → automated, ten tools → one)  
- ✅ Green: Still in production, still used, or became the standard  
- ❌ Red: "My manager assigned it to me" (not initiative)  
- ❌ Red: No metrics or evidence ("it was great")  
- ❌ Red: You did it alone and didn't involve anyone (shows no collaboration)

## Questions to ask them

Use 2–3 of these in the recruiter screen, and 2–3 in the behavioral round. Tune them once you have a company name and job description.

1. **"What does success look like for this role in the first six months?"**  
   (Recruiter or hiring manager. Tells you how they measure performance and what the ramp expectation is.)

2. **"What's the biggest technical challenge the team is facing right now?"**  
   (Hiring manager or eng interviewer. Shows you care about the work, and you'll learn if it's a problem you want to solve.)

3. **"How does the team balance shipping new features and paying down tech debt?"**  
   (Hiring manager or senior IC. Reveals team health, velocity, and whether you'll spend all your time firefighting.)

4. **"Can you walk me through a recent project this team shipped, and how decisions were made?"**  
   (Hiring manager. Tells you how much autonomy ICs have, how product and eng collaborate, and whether the team ships.)

5. **"What do people who succeed here have in common?"**  
   (Recruiter or hiring manager. Useful culture and expectation signal—do they say "work long hours," "move fast," "ask great questions," "collaborate well"?)

If you get a company name and job description, replace question 2 or 4 with something specific to their product or stack (e.g., "How does the team use [TypeScript/React/Node] day to day?" or "I saw the product does [X]—how does that work under the hood?").
