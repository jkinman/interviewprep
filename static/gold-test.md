# Interview prep cheat sheet

*Tailored for Acme Corp — senior full-stack, recruiter phone screen (30 min).*

---

## Part A — Top 5 HR / recruiter screen questions

#### A1. Tell me about yourself / walk me through your background

- **What they are really screening for:** Can you communicate clearly and show a coherent career arc that leads logically to this senior full-stack role? Are you at the right level, and do you sound like someone the hiring manager will want to talk to?

- **Tailored answer:**  
  *I'm a senior full-stack engineer with eight years building production systems in React, TypeScript, and Node. I'm currently at Highspot, where I designed and built an internal LLM gateway that routes requests across models and powers AI features the product team ships. Before that I was at Article, where I built a Figma-to-code pipeline that let designers generate React components automatically, and I shipped more than ten internal apps. Earlier at Appnovation I built a COVID tracking system that four provinces deployed. I'm Vancouver-based and authorized to work in Canada. I'm looking now because I want to join a team where I can keep building full-stack systems at scale, and Acme's senior full-stack role looks like a great fit.*

- **Pitfalls:**  
  - Starting with your college major or going back more than eight years unless asked.  
  - Listing every technology instead of the coherent thread (React/TypeScript/Node, internal tooling, production systems).  
  - Forgetting to say *why now* and *why this company* at the end.  
  - Running past 90 seconds—this is a summary, not a project deep-dive.

---

#### A2. Why are you leaving, or why are you looking now?

- **What they are really screening for:** Are you running away from something (performance, conflict, burnout), or running toward something? Will you be stable if hired, or is this a rebound role?

- **Tailored answer:**  
  *I've been at Highspot for about three years, and I'm proud of what I built—the LLM gateway is in production and the AI features are live. I'm looking now because I want my next role to have more scope on the full-stack side, ideally where I can shape architecture and ship customer-facing features, not just internal infrastructure. Acme's focus on [ADD DETAIL: specific product area or engineering culture from the job description or your research] is exactly the kind of environment where I do my best work.*

- **Pitfalls:**  
  - Saying you're "bored" or that the company is "going downhill."  
  - Being vague ("just ready for a change").  
  - Implying you're desperate or that you'll take any offer.  
  - Skipping the "why *this* company" anchor at the end.

---

#### A3. Why this company / why this role?

- **What they are really screening for:** Did you apply to 200 jobs with one click, or do you actually know what Acme does and care about the problem? Will you accept an offer if extended, or are you just shopping?

- **Tailored answer:**  
  *I'm drawn to Acme because [ADD DETAIL: specific product, mission, technical challenge, or team structure you care about—e.g., "you're building X for Y customers," "the engineering blog shows you invest in TypeScript and modern tooling," "the team is small enough that a senior engineer can still shape architecture"]. The senior full-stack role maps directly to what I've been doing—React and Node in production, internal tooling, and now AI integrations—and I want to work somewhere I can take ownership of features end-to-end. That's what this role looks like to me.*

- **Pitfalls:**  
  - Generic praise ("Acme is a great company," "I've heard good things").  
  - Only talking about what you'll learn, not what you'll contribute.  
  - Repeating the job description back without connecting it to your actual experience.  
  - Forgetting to mention the *role* fit, not just the company fit.

---

#### A4. What are your salary expectations?

- **What they are really screening for:** Are you within budget? Will you waste the team's time if the range doesn't match? Are you going to lowball yourself or demand a number that signals you're at a different level?

- **Tailored answer:**  
  *[ADD DETAIL: your target range in CAD, e.g., "I'm looking for 140 to 160K CAD base"]. That's based on my eight years of experience, the senior level of this role, and the Vancouver market. I'm flexible within a reasonable range if the total comp and the role are a strong fit, and I'm happy to talk through the full package—equity, benefits, bonus structure—once we're aligned on the role itself.*

- **Pitfalls:**  
  - Saying "I'm open to anything" or "whatever is fair"—it signals you don't know your worth or the market.  
  - Giving a single number with no flexibility.  
  - Anchoring too low out of fear, or too high without evidence.  
  - Forgetting to mention currency (USD vs. CAD matters).

---

#### A5. Logistics: availability, start date, work authorization, location, and work model

- **What they are really screening for:** Can you legally work where the role is based? Can you start soon enough? Are there any logistical blockers (visa, relocation, remote/onsite mismatch) that will kill the process later?

- **Tailored answer:**  
  *I'm authorized to work in Canada and I'm based in Vancouver. I can start [ADD DETAIL: your notice period or availability, e.g., "in three weeks" or "immediately"]. I'm comfortable with [ADD DETAIL: the work model Acme uses—remote, hybrid, onsite—or state your preference if the job description allows flexibility]. No visa sponsorship needed, no relocation required.*

- **Pitfalls:**  
  - Being vague about work authorization ("I think I can work in Canada").  
  - Saying you need six months' notice without explaining why.  
  - Assuming the role is remote if the job description says hybrid or onsite.  
  - Forgetting to confirm your location if the company has geo restrictions.

## Part B — Top 5 behavioral questions

#### B1. Tell me about your biggest failure (or a time the plan broke)

- **Competency this is coded against:** Learning agility, ownership, resilience, and self-awareness. The interviewer wants to see that you can admit mistakes, extract a lesson, and change your behavior. A 4/4 answer shows you owned the failure, didn't deflect blame, and applied the learning to a later situation.

- **What a strong (4/4) answer looks like:**  
  - A real failure you owned (missed deadline, wrong technical choice, broken assumption).  
  - Concrete actions *you* took to fix it or mitigate the damage.  
  - A result that shows the outcome (what broke, what you salvaged).  
  - A credible lesson and evidence you changed your process later.

- **What a weak (1/4) answer looks like:**  
  - "I don't really have failures" or a humblebrag ("I care too much").  
  - Blaming the team, the PM, the manager, or the customer.  
  - Vague "we learned and moved on" with no specifics.  
  - A story that isn't a failure (a challenge you overcame easily).

- **Green flags / red flags:**  
  - **Green:** You say "I misjudged" or "I should have," you name the consequence, you show the fix and the new habit.  
  - **Red:** You blame others, minimize the failure, claim it wasn't your fault, or offer no lesson.

- **STAR answer:**  
  - **Situation:** [ADD DETAIL: describe a project or feature at Highspot, Article, or Appnovation where something went wrong—missed deadline, wrong architecture, broken release, scope creep].  
  - **Task:** [ADD DETAIL: what you were responsible for delivering, and what the expectation was].  
  - **Action:** [ADD DETAIL: what you did that caused or contributed to the failure, and then what you did to recover—communicate to stakeholders, roll back, pivot, ask for help].  
  - **Result:** [ADD DETAIL: the outcome—what shipped late, what you salvaged, what the customer or team experienced].  
  - **Lesson:** [ADD DETAIL: the specific change you made afterward—new testing process, earlier design review, more frequent check-ins, better scoping].

  **[ADD DETAIL: no confirmed failure story in the input—add facts before using this in an interview. Possible threads: Highspot LLM gateway (complexity, integration challenges?), Article Figma pipeline (technical debt, edge cases?), Appnovation COVID system (tight timeline, production bugs?). Choose one and add the specific failure, your role, the consequence, and the lesson.]**

- **Likely follow-up probe:**  
  *"What would you do differently if you faced that situation again?"*  
  Walk through the new process or decision you'd make, and anchor it in the lesson from the STAR. If you've used that new process since, name the project.

#### B2. Tell me about a conflict with a coworker

- **Competency this is coded against:** Collaboration, communication, conflict resolution, and emotional intelligence. The interviewer wants to see that you can disagree professionally, seek to understand the other person's perspective, and reach a productive outcome without burning relationships.

- **What a strong (4/4) answer looks like:**  
  - A real disagreement (technical approach, priority, process, ownership).  
  - You listened, stated your reasoning, and found common ground or escalated appropriately.  
  - The outcome preserved or strengthened the working relationship.  
  - You reflect on what you learned about the other person's constraints or perspective.

- **What a weak (1/4) answer looks like:**  
  - "I've never had a conflict" or "everyone loves working with me."  
  - You bad-mouth the coworker or imply they were incompetent.  
  - You "won" the argument and they "lost," with no reflection.  
  - Vague "we talked it out" with no specifics about the disagreement or resolution.

- **Green flags / red flags:**  
  - **Green:** You name the other person's valid concern, you proposed a compromise, you followed up afterward.  
  - **Red:** You say "they were wrong," you went around them, you never resolved it, you hold a grudge.

- **STAR answer:**  
  - **Situation:** [ADD DETAIL: a project or decision at Highspot, Article, or Appnovation where you and a coworker disagreed—technical design, priority, ownership, process].  
  - **Task:** [ADD DETAIL: what you both needed to deliver, and why the disagreement mattered].  
  - **Action:** [ADD DETAIL: how you surfaced the conflict, what you each argued, how you listened, whether you compromised or escalated, what you agreed to try].  
  - **Result:** [ADD DETAIL: the outcome—did the solution work, did the relationship stay intact, did you work together again].

  **[ADD DETAIL: no confirmed conflict story in the input—add facts before using this in an interview. Possible threads: Highspot (cross-team integration, design disagreement on the LLM gateway?), Article (designer vs. engineer tension on the Figma pipeline?), Appnovation (tight timeline, priority conflict on the COVID system?). Choose one and add the disagreement, your role, the resolution, and the outcome.]**

- **Likely follow-up probe:**  
  *"What did you learn about working with that person, or about handling disagreements?"*  
  Name the other person's constraint or perspective you didn't see at first, and the communication habit you adopted afterward.

#### B3. Tell me about a time you disagreed with a manager, or pushed back

- **Competency this is coded against:** Judgment, communication, and courage. The interviewer wants to see that you can challenge authority respectfully, that you bring data or reasoning (not just opinion), and that you can commit even if you don't get your way.

- **What a strong (4/4) answer looks like:**  
  - A real disagreement (technical decision, priority, timeline, scope).  
  - You prepared your reasoning, brought options, and stated the risk clearly.  
  - The outcome shows either you influenced the decision, or you committed and made it work.  
  - You reflect on what you learned about the manager's constraints or the broader context.

- **What a weak (1/4) answer looks like:**  
  - "I've never disagreed with a manager" or "I always do what I'm told."  
  - You bad-mouth the manager or imply they were incompetent.  
  - You pushed back and then quietly sabotaged the decision.  
  - Vague "I raised my concern" with no specifics about the reasoning or outcome.

- **Green flags / red flags:**  
  - **Green:** You framed it as a question or option, you accepted the final call, you delivered anyway.  
  - **Red:** You say "they wouldn't listen," you went around them, you say "I was right and they were wrong" with no nuance.

- **STAR answer:**  
  - **Situation:** [ADD DETAIL: a project or decision at Highspot, Article, or Appnovation where your manager made a call you disagreed with—timeline, technical approach, priority, scope].  
  - **Task:** [ADD DETAIL: what you were responsible for, and why the decision affected your work or the outcome].  
  - **Action:** [ADD DETAIL: how you raised the concern, what data or reasoning you brought, what you proposed instead, whether you got buy-in or committed to the manager's call].  
  - **Result:** [ADD DETAIL: the outcome—did your approach get adopted, did the manager's call work, did you make it work anyway, what did you learn about their constraints].

  **[ADD DETAIL: no confirmed pushback story in the input—add facts before using this in an interview. Possible threads: Highspot (manager wanted to rush LLM gateway launch, you flagged risk?), Article (manager prioritized a different internal app, you made the case for the Figma pipeline?), Appnovation (manager set an aggressive timeline for the COVID system?). Choose one and add the disagreement, your reasoning, the manager's response, and the outcome.]**

- **Likely follow-up probe:**  
  *"If you could do it again, would you push back the same way, or would you change your approach?"*  
  Reflect on whether you had the right data, whether you should have escalated or committed sooner, and what you learned about framing disagreements.

#### B4. Tell me about a tough deadline, or a time you had to deliver through ambiguity

- **Competency this is coded against:** Bias for action, prioritization, ownership, and comfort with uncertainty. The interviewer wants to see that you can scope fast, make progress without perfect information, and ship something valuable under pressure.

- **What a strong (4/4) answer looks like:**  
  - A real tight timeline or unclear requirement (product launch, production incident, stakeholder shift).  
  - You broke the work into pieces, made trade-offs, and communicated the risks.  
  - You shipped on time (or close), and the result had measurable impact.  
  - You reflect on what you learned about scoping or managing ambiguity.

- **What a weak (1/4) answer looks like:**  
  - "I just worked nights and weekends" with no trade-off or prioritization.  
  - Blaming the PM, the manager, or the customer for bad requirements.  
  - Vague "we figured it out" with no specifics about what you did.  
  - You missed the deadline and frame it as someone else's fault.

- **Green flags / red flags:**  
  - **Green:** You scoped an MVP, you asked clarifying questions, you shipped iteratively, you communicated risk early.  
  - **Red:** You say "I pulled all-nighters," you complain about the deadline, you delivered late with no learning.

- **STAR answer:**  
  - **Situation:** At Appnovation I built a COVID tracking system that four provinces deployed. The timeline was extremely tight because the pandemic was moving fast and governments needed real-time data.  
  - **Task:** I was responsible for designing and building the full-stack system—React front-end, Node back-end, and integration with provincial health data sources. The requirement was high-level at the start: track cases, display trends, make it accessible to the public.  
  - **Action:** I scoped the MVP with the product lead and the client: core tracking, a clean dashboard, and one data integration per province. I pushed back on feature requests that would have delayed launch—reporting, mobile app, admin tooling—and committed to v2. I built in two-week sprints, tested with one province first, then rolled out to the other three. I communicated the trade-offs every week: what we'd ship, what we'd defer, and what the risk was if we added scope.  
  - **Result:** We launched in [ADD DETAIL: timeframe, e.g., six weeks]. All four provinces deployed it in production, and it handled [ADD DETAIL: usage, e.g., thousands of daily visitors or public-facing traffic] during the first wave. The client came back for the v2 features we'd deferred. I learned that scoping the MVP hard and communicating trade-offs early is the only way to ship under pressure without burning out the team.

- **Likely follow-up probe:**  
  *"What did you defer or cut, and how did you decide what stayed in?"*  
  Walk through the prioritization: what had to work day one (core tracking, one integration), what the client could live without (reporting, mobile, admin tooling), and how you validated that call with the product lead and the client.

#### B5. Tell me about your proudest achievement, or a time you led without formal authority

- **Competency this is coded against:** Initiative, influence, technical leadership, and impact. The interviewer wants to see that you can drive outcomes without being told, that you can bring others along, and that you deliver results that matter to the business or the team.

- **What a strong (4/4) answer looks like:**  
  - A project or initiative you started, or a problem you solved that wasn't assigned.  
  - You identified the opportunity, built buy-in, and coordinated across people or teams.  
  - The result had measurable impact (shipped feature, cost savings, team velocity, user outcome).  
  - You reflect on what made it successful and what you'd do next time.

- **What a weak (1/4) answer looks like:**  
  - "I did my job really well" with no initiative or leadership.  
  - Vague "the team was happy" with no concrete result.  
  - You take credit for someone else's idea or work.  
  - You say "I" but the story is all "we" with no clear action from you.

- **Green flags / red flags:**  
  - **Green:** You name the problem, you built consensus, you drove the work, you shipped and measured the result.  
  - **Red:** You claim sole credit, you skip the result, you say "it was easy," you don't explain the leadership piece.

- **STAR answer:**  
  - **Situation:** At Article, the design team was spending hours manually translating Figma mockups into React components, and engineers were spending just as much time fixing drift between design and code.  
  - **Task:** I saw the opportunity to automate it. No one had asked me to build a Figma-to-code pipeline, but I knew it would save both teams time and reduce bugs.  
  - **Action:** I pitched the idea to the design lead and the engineering manager, showed a proof-of-concept in a week, and got buy-in to productionize it. I built the pipeline in TypeScript and Node—it parsed Figma's API, generated React components with Tailwind classes, and committed them to our monorepo. I onboarded the design team, wrote docs, and iterated based on their feedback. I also built ten-plus internal apps using the pipeline to prove it worked at scale.  
  - **Result:** Designers could ship components to engineers in minutes instead of hours. Engineering estimates for UI work dropped [ADD DETAIL: measurable time savings, e.g., "by 30%" or "from days to hours"]. The pipeline became the standard workflow for new features. I'm proud of it because I drove it end-to-end—from idea to production adoption—and because it improved collaboration between two teams that had friction before.

- **Likely follow-up probe:**  
  *"What was the hardest part of getting buy-in or driving adoption?"*  
  Name the objection or concern (designers worried about losing control, engineers worried about code quality, managers worried about maintenance). Explain how you addressed it (proof-of-concept, iteration, docs, training, showing the time savings with real data).

### Questions to ask them

1. **"What does success look like for a senior full-stack engineer in the first six months at Acme?"**  
   Listen for onboarding expectations, the first project, and how performance is measured. This tells you if the role is well-scoped and if the team has a clear ramp plan.

2. **"How does the engineering team use React, TypeScript, and Node in production today, and are there parts of the stack you're planning to evolve?"**  
   This shows you care about the technical environment and gives you a preview of technical debt, modernization, or greenfield work.

3. **"What's the balance between building new features and maintaining or refactoring existing systems for a senior engineer on this team?"**  
   Reveals whether you'll be doing greenfield work, firefighting, or a mix—and whether the team values sustainability or just ships fast.

4. **"How does the team collaborate with product and design, and how much ownership does a senior engineer have over technical decisions?"**  
   Tests for autonomy, process maturity, and whether "senior" means you're a code monkey or a technical leader.

5. **"What are the biggest technical or organizational challenges the team is facing right now?"**  
   This is your chance to hear the unvarnished truth about what's hard—hiring, tech debt, shifting priorities, scaling—and decide if you want to work on those problems.
