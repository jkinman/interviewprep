## B1. Tell me about your biggest failure (or a time the plan broke)

**Competency this is coded against:** Learning agility, ownership, resilience, judgment. Interviewers are looking for evidence that you can name a real mistake, own it without blame, and show what you learned or changed. A 4/4 answer proves you operate at the senior level—you see systems, not just tasks, and you course-correct.

**What a strong (4/4) answer looks like:**
- A real failure with consequences (missed deadline, broke production, wrong technical choice, conflict that hurt the team)
- You own your part—no "the PM didn't tell me" or "the manager should have caught it"
- A clear action you took to fix it or mitigate damage
- A credible lesson or behavior change (not "I learned to communicate better"—something specific you do now that you didn't do then)

**What a weak (1/4) answer looks like:**
- No real failure—"I worked too hard" or "I cared too much"
- Blame—"my manager didn't give me the context" or "the team wasn't aligned"
- No result or fix—the story just ends with "it was hard"
- No learning, or a generic learning ("I learned teamwork is important")

**Green flags / red flags:**
- ✅ Green: You name the mistake in the first 10 seconds. You say "I should have" not "they should have." You describe a concrete change in how you work now.
- ✕ Red: You avoid the word "failure." You blame someone else. You say "there was nothing I could have done differently." You pick a story where you were the hero.

**STAR answer:**

**Situation:** At Highspot, during the AI transformation, I was tasked with designing an internal LLM gateway to centralize model access across teams.

**Task:** I needed to ship something fast—teams were blocked—but I also needed it to scale and be maintainable. I made the call to build a simple passthrough service first and add features later.

**Action:** I shipped the MVP in two weeks. It worked. But I didn't build in enough observability or rate-limiting because I was focused on speed. Three weeks later, one team's script hit the gateway in a loop and racked up a huge OpenAI bill before anyone noticed. I had to explain to leadership why we didn't have safeguards.

I owned it. I added rate-limiting, cost alerts, and a dashboard showing usage by team. I also set up a weekly sync with the teams using the gateway so I could catch issues early instead of reacting.

**Result:** The gateway stayed in production and became the standard for all LLM access. We didn't have another cost incident. I also changed how I scope MVPs—I now build in basic observability and guardrails even in v1, because "ship fast" doesn't mean "ship blind."

**Spoken draft (about 90 seconds):**

At Highspot, I was building an internal LLM gateway during the company's AI transformation. Teams were blocked waiting for centralized model access, so I shipped an MVP in two weeks—just a simple passthrough service, no bells and whistles. It worked. Teams unblocked.

But I didn't build in rate-limiting or cost alerts because I was focused on speed. Three weeks later, one team's script hit the gateway in a loop and racked up a huge OpenAI bill before anyone noticed. I had to go to leadership and explain why we didn't have safeguards.

**Likely follow-up probe:** "What would you have done differently if you could go back?"

**How to answer:** Say: "I would have added basic rate-limiting and a cost alert from day one. It would have added maybe two days to the build, but it would have saved us the incident and the trust hit with leadership. I still would have shipped fast, but I would have defined 'MVP' to include those guardrails, not treat them as v2 features."

## B2. Tell me about a conflict with a coworker

**Competency this is coded against:** Collaboration, conflict resolution, communication, empathy, judgment. Interviewers want to see that you can disagree professionally, that you don't avoid conflict or escalate unnecessarily, and that you can find a path forward even when you don't get your way.

**What a strong (4/4) answer looks like:**
- A real disagreement with stakes (not "we had different coding styles")
- You engaged directly with the person, not around them
- You listened, you made your case with data or reasoning, and you found a resolution (compromise, escalation, or you changed your mind)
- The relationship stayed intact or improved

**What a weak (1/4) answer looks like:**
- "I don't really have conflicts" or "I get along with everyone"
- You bad-mouth the coworker or imply they were incompetent
- You went around them (talked to their manager first, or just did it your way)
- No resolution—the story ends with "we agreed to disagree"

**Green flags / red flags:**
- ✅ Green: You name the disagreement clearly. You say "I listened" or "I asked why they felt that way." You describe a specific resolution. You say "we" not "I won."
- ✕ Red: You say "they were wrong." You say "I had to escalate because they wouldn't listen." You avoid naming what you disagreed about. You say "it worked out" without explaining how.

**STAR answer:**

**Situation:** At Article, I was building an automated ETL pipeline that turned Figma exports into themed TypeScript plugins for our component system.

**Task:** A senior backend engineer on the platform team wanted me to use their existing build pipeline instead of building a separate Figma-to-code tool. They argued it would reduce duplication and make maintenance easier.

**Action:** I disagreed because the Figma export format was unstable and the design team's workflow didn't fit the backend pipeline's assumptions. I set up a 30-minute call with the engineer. I walked them through the Figma export structure and showed them three examples where the backend pipeline would break. I also acknowledged their point about duplication—I said I'd document the tool and keep it simple so it wouldn't become a maintenance burden.

They still weren't convinced, so I proposed a compromise: I'd build the Figma tool as a standalone script, but I'd use their pipeline's config format so we could merge them later if it made sense. They agreed.

**Result:** I shipped the Figma ETL pipeline. It worked for 10+ internal apps. The backend engineer and I stayed on good terms—we worked together on two other projects after that. And six months later, we did end up merging part of the Figma tool into their pipeline, because by then the Figma export format had stabilized.

**How to answer:** Say: "If they'd said no, I would have asked to escalate to our shared manager—not as 'I need you to overrule them,' but as 'we need a decision on this because we're blocked.' I would have laid out both options, the tradeoffs, and what I'd need to make their approach work. I wouldn't have just gone around them or built it in secret."

## B4. Tell me about a tough deadline, or a time you had to deliver through ambiguity

**Competency this is coded against:** Bias for action, ownership, judgment, resilience, prioritization. Interviewers want to see that you can ship under pressure without cutting corners that matter, and that you can operate without perfect clarity.

**What a strong (4/4) answer looks like:**
- A real deadline or ambiguous situation with stakes (not "I worked late one week")
- You made a clear prioritization call or scoped the problem yourself
- You communicated risk or tradeoffs to stakeholders
- You shipped on time, and the result was good enough (not perfect, but good enough)

**What a weak (1/4) answer looks like:**
- You say "I just worked harder" with no prioritization story
- You cut corners that broke things later, and you don't acknowledge it
- You blame the deadline or the ambiguity instead of owning the outcome
- No result, or the result was "we missed the deadline but I tried"

**Green flags / red flags:**
- ✅ Green: You say "I scoped it down to X because Y was nice-to-have." You say "I told stakeholders we'd ship A but not B." You say "I asked clarifying questions to reduce ambiguity."
- ✕ Red: You say "I worked 80-hour weeks." You say "the deadline was unrealistic." You say "I shipped it but it was buggy and we had to redo it."

**STAR answer:**

**Situation:** At Highspot, I was building an AI-generated lesson wizard—a customer-facing feature using React and TypeScript—during a tight release window tied to a customer conference.

**Task:** Product wanted the full wizard (multi-step form, AI generation, preview, edit, save) in three weeks. That was ambitious given the LLM integration, the frontend complexity, and the feature-flag rollout we needed for safety.

**Action:** I scoped it. I told product: "We can ship the core flow—generate, preview, save—in three weeks. But the edit step and the advanced options will be v2, or we'll miss the conference." They agreed.

I broke the work into vertical slices: LLM integration first, then the simplest UI that could call it, then the feature flag, then polish. I also set up a daily 10-minute sync with the PM and the designer so we could course-correct fast if something wasn't working.

I hit one ambiguity: the LLM prompt structure wasn't defined. I didn't wait for a spec. I wrote three prompt variations, tested them with real content, and brought data to the PM. We picked one and moved on.

**Result:** We shipped the wizard on time for the conference. It worked. Customers used it in the demo. We added the edit step two weeks later. The lesson I took: when the deadline is real, scope is your friend. Cut features, not quality.

## B5. Tell me about your proudest achievement, or a time you led without formal authority

**Competency this is coded against:** Ownership, leadership, impact, influence, initiative. Interviewers want to see that you can drive outcomes beyond your immediate scope, that you can bring people along without a title, and that you measure success by impact not effort.

**What a strong (4/4) answer looks like:**
- A real achievement with measurable impact (not "I learned a lot")
- You initiated it or drove it—it wasn't handed to you
- You influenced people who didn't report to you (other teams, senior engineers, managers, designers)
- A clear result: shipped, adopted, scaled, saved time/money, unblocked others

**What a weak (1/4) answer looks like:**
- You say "I worked really hard" but no result
- You take credit for a team effort without acknowledging the team
- The achievement is just doing your job (not above and beyond)
- No influence story—you worked alone or only with your direct team

**Green flags / red flags:**
- ✅ Green: You say "I proposed this" or "I saw the gap and built it." You say "I brought in X team" or "I convinced Y person." You quantify impact (users, teams, time saved, cost avoided).
- ✕ Red: You say "we did this" without clarifying your role. You claim impact you didn't have (revenue, users) without evidence. You say "I'm proud of everything I've done."

**STAR answer:**
