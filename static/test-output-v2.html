Part A — Top 5 HR / Recruiter Screen Questions

A1. Tell me about yourself / walk me through your background

What they are really screening for:

Can you tell a clear, chronological story that explains your choices and shows you're at the right level? Do you communicate concisely and confidently?

Tailored answer:

Opening line: "I'm a senior full-stack engineer with eight years building production systems in React, TypeScript, and Node."

I've spent the last eight years as a full-stack engineer working in React, TypeScript, and Node. Most recently at Highspot from 2023 to early this year, I built an LLM gateway and AI features that brought ML capabilities into the product. Before that at Article I owned a Figma-to-code pipeline and built over ten internal apps that helped non-engineers ship faster. Earlier at Appnovation I built a COVID tracking system deployed across four provinces during the pandemic. I'm looking for my next senior IC role where I can keep solving hard problems at the intersection of infrastructure and product.

Pitfalls:

  • Going back to college or irrelevant early jobs—start at Appnovation or later
  • Listing every tech you've touched instead of anchoring on React/TS/Node
  • Rambling past 90 seconds—this is a 30-minute call
  • Ending without a forward-looking sentence (why you're interviewing)

A2. Why are you leaving, or why are you looking now?

What they are really screening for:

Are you a flight risk, running from a problem, or making a thoughtful move? Is there a red flag (performance, conflict, job-hopping)?

Tailored answer:

Opening line: "Highspot was a great place to work, but the role ended in early 2026 and I'm ready for the next challenge."

I was at Highspot until early this year—loved the team and the work on AI features, but the role came to an end. I'm looking for a senior IC role where I can keep building full-stack systems that touch both infrastructure and user-facing product. After eight years I know I do my best work when I'm solving ambiguous problems and shipping fast, so I'm looking for a team that values that.

Pitfalls:

  • Being vague about why you left—"the role ended" is neutral and factual, use it
  • Bad-mouthing Highspot, Article, or any past employer
  • Saying "I want a new challenge" without grounding it in what you actually want to do
  • Oversharing personal reasons or sounding desperate

Part B — Top 5 Behavioral Questions

B1. Tell me about your biggest failure (or a time the plan broke)

Competency this is coded against:

Learning agility, ownership, humility. Interviewers want to see that you can admit a mistake, diagnose what went wrong, and change your behavior. A strong answer shows you owned the outcome (not just "the team failed"), identified the root cause, and applied the lesson later. Weak answers blame others or claim no real failure.

What a strong (4/4) answer looks like:

  • A concrete example with a real negative outcome you owned
  • Diagnosis of what you personally did wrong (missed a dependency, didn't validate assumptions, didn't communicate risk)
  • What you did to fix it, and what you changed going forward
  • Appropriate scope for senior IC (a technical decision, a project plan, a cross-team dependency—not a trivial bug)

What a weak (1/4) answer looks like:

  • "I've never really failed" or a story with no real consequence
  • Blaming the PM, the manager, or "the business" for changing requirements
  • Vague "we" language that hides your role
  • No reflection or lesson—just describing chaos

Green flags:

  • Owned the mistake in first person ("I underestimated," "I didn't check")
  • Showed you fixed it and applied the lesson to a later project
  • Demonstrated accountability without self-flagellation

Red flags:

  • Bad-mouthing the team or the company
  • Picking a failure that wasn't really yours
  • No learning or behavior change

  • You sought to understand their perspective before pushing yours
  • You found common ground or a data-driven way to decide

  • Villain-izing the other person ("they were impossible")
  • Winning by pulling rank or going over their head without trying to resolve it first
  • No resolution or learning

Green flags:

  • Showed curiosity about the other person's constraints or goals
  • Used data or a prototype to test competing ideas
  • Escalated thoughtfully if needed, with the other person's knowledge
  • Maintained a respectful tone in the retelling

Red flags:

  • Bad-mouthing the coworker
  • Claiming you "won" without compromise
  • No attempt to understand their view
  • Passive-aggressive or avoidant behavior

B3. Tell me about a time you disagreed with a manager, or pushed back

Competency this is coded against:

Judgment, backbone, communication, respect for authority. Interviewers want to see that you can challenge a decision respectfully, with data or a clear alternative, and that you disagree-and-commit if the manager decides differently. They're checking for insubordination, stubbornness, or inability to influence upward.

What a strong (4/4) answer looks like:

  • A real technical or strategic disagreement with a manager, not a minor quibble
  • You brought data, a proposal, or a risk the manager hadn't considered
  • You made your case respectfully and accepted the final call
  • The outcome was better because you spoke up, or you learned why your view was incomplete

What a weak (1/4) answer looks like:

  • Claiming you've never disagreed with a manager
  • Framing it as the manager being wrong and you being right, with no nuance
  • Going around the manager or bad-mouthing them afterward
  • No learning or acknowledgment that the manager had context you didn't

Green flags:

  • Framed the disagreement as "here's the risk I see" or "here's an alternative," not "you're wrong"
  • Listened to the manager's reasoning and updated your view or disagreed-and-committed
  • Followed up with results or new data to revisit the decision if needed

Red flags:

  • Disrespectful tone or public challenge
  • No attempt to understand the manager's constraints (budget, political, timeline)
  • Holding a grudge or undermining the decision after it was made

B4. Tell me about a tough deadline, or a time you had to deliver through ambiguity

Competency this is coded against:

Bias for action, prioritization, ownership, resilience. Interviewers want proof you can ship under pressure, clarify ambiguous requirements, and make pragmatic trade-offs without over-engineering or waiting for perfect clarity. They're checking for decision paralysis, blame, or scope creep.

What a strong (4/4) answer looks like:

  • A real high-pressure project (tight deadline, unclear requirements, or shifting constraints)
  • You clarified the must-haves, cut scope intelligently, and unblocked yourself
  • You shipped on time (or close) with acceptable quality
  • You communicated risk and trade-offs transparently

What a weak (1/4) answer looks like:

  • Vague story with no real deadline pressure or no shipped result
  • Blaming the PM or business for "bad requirements" without showing how you helped clarify them
  • Claiming you delivered "everything perfectly" with no trade-offs—unlikely and not credible
  • No reflection on what you'd do differently

Green flags:

  • Showed how you clarified the goal and scoped the MVP
  • Made smart trade-offs (manual steps, tech debt, reduced scope) to hit the date
  • Communicated status and risk proactively
  • Named what you'd do differently with more time

Red flags:

  • Blaming others for the pressure
  • No evidence of prioritization—just "worked harder"
  • Shipped something broken or ignored quality entirely
  • Passive voice ("we were told to ship")—where was your agency?

B5. Tell me about your proudest achievement, or a time you led without formal authority

Competency this is coded against:

Impact, ownership, influence, technical leadership. Interviewers want to see the biggest lever you've pulled, the complexity you navigated, and how you drove results without being a manager. They're checking for scope appropriate to senior IC (not just "I fixed a bug"), and whether you can articulate impact in business or user terms.

What a strong (4/4) answer looks like:

  • A high-impact project you owned end-to-end (or led a significant piece of)
  • Clear business or user outcome (revenue, scale, efficiency, unblocking other engineers)
  • You influenced others (design, PM, other engineers) without formal authority
  • Appropriate technical complexity for senior IC

What a weak (1/4) answer looks like:

  • Vague claims ("I improved performance by a lot") with no numbers
  • Taking credit for a team effort with no clear personal contribution
  • Junior-level scope (a single bug fix or a small feature) presented as a big win
  • No business or user impact—just "it was hard"

Green flags:

  • Quantified impact (users, provinces served, number of apps, adoption, latency, error rate)
  • Showed how you influenced design, product, or other engineers
  • Described the technical challenge and how you solved it
  • Gave credit to collaborators while owning your piece

Red flags:

  • Taking sole credit for a team project
  • No measurable outcome
  • Overstating your role (saying "I" when it was "we")
  • Picking something trivial or too far back

Questions to Ask Them

Since no specific company or job description was provided, here are strong general questions for a senior IC in a recruiter screen. Tailor them once you know the company.

  1. "What does success look like in the first six months for this role?"
    Reveals priorities, expectations, and whether they have a clear onboarding plan.
  2. "How does the team balance speed and quality—when do you cut scope vs. when do you invest in the right architecture?"
    Tests for pragmatism and whether they value thoughtful trade-offs or just "ship fast."
  3. "What's the team structure, and how do engineers collaborate with product and design?"
    Reveals autonomy, communication culture, and cross-functional dynamics.
  4. "What's the biggest technical challenge the team is facing right now?"
    Gives you a sense of complexity and whether the role is well-scoped or still being defined.
  5. "What's your interview process from here, and what should I prepare for in the next round?"
    Practical and shows you're serious. Also helps you prep better.
